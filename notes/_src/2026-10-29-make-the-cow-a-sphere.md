<p><img loading="lazy" src="/uploads/notes/make-the-cow-a-sphere/f75b0aaf8031adefe5b2319f998d2454.jpg" alt="A cartoon spherical cow in a field, next to the title Make the cow a sphere"></p><p>There's a joke every physics undergrad hears in their first semester, and I've been using it at work for about fifteen years.</p>
<p>A dairy farmer's cows stop giving milk. He's desperate, so he calls the university and they send over a physicist. The physicist walks the farm, takes a bunch of notes, disappears for a week, and comes back with a stack of paper. "I've solved it," she says, "but the solution only works for spherical cows in a vacuum."</p>
<p>Everybody laughs at the physicist, but the thing is, she's right. Not about the cows of course, but about the method. You can't solve the real cow - it has legs and it moos and has all manner of complicating factors. So you make the cow a sphere, solve the sphere, and then put the legs back on one at a time and see which ones actually change the answer. This is the technique I've used on every client problem I've ever been handed, so I figured I'd finally write it down to share the dairy-driven decision-making directions with you.</p>
<h2>Where it came from</h2>
<p>I studied physics at WashU, specifically the theoretical/mathematical side of things. The thing that program drilled into me harder than any specific equation was a habit of sizing a problem before solving it. How big should the answer roughly be? Which part of the problem is doing most of the work? What can I throw away for now? Physicists call the big part the leading order, and everything else is a correction you come back for later, if it ends up mattering at all.</p>
<p>Then I spent some time at Slalom Consulting and learned the business-y half, which is three questions: What/ So what/ Now what? <br></p><ul><li>What: What actually happened, in unbiased terms?</li><li>So What: What does it mean for the person you're speaking to?</li><li>Now What: What is going to happen to fix the problem or keep the success up?</li></ul><p></p><p>Physics is extremely good at the first question and has literally nothing to say about the other two. Consulting is great at the other two and will cheerfully skip the first one if the slide looks nice enough (tbh I made plenty of lipstick-on-a-pig slides that stretched the supportingdata pretty thin).</p>
<p>So I mashed them together into a 4-step-process I call the result Leading Order, because I'm tired of coming up with names and that one fits.</p>
<h2>The four steps</h2>
<h3>1. Size it before you solve it</h3>
<p>Before I do anything else I want a rough number for how big the thing is, and I want it fast and I want it ugly. Enrico Fermi was the best there ever was at this. He famously estimated the yield of the first atomic bomb test by dropping scraps of paper as the blast wave went past and watching how far they travelled, and he got within a factor of two of the real answer from what was basically confetti. The point was never precision, it's that once you know the answer is "hundreds" and not "tens of thousands," half of the possible explanations die on the spot and you can stop thinking about them.</p>
<p>In practice this means I'll sit with a client problem for 5-10 minutes with nothing but a notepad and ask: roughly how much money is this? Roughly how many customers? Is this a 5% problem or a 50% problem? You would be shocked how often the answer to that one question ends the meeting.</p>
<h3>2. Make the cow a sphere</h3>
<p>Now I strip everything out of the problem that isn't the leading term, and I mean everything, until I have a toy version that is embarrassingly simple. There's a famous study behind this. In 1981, Michelene Chi and her colleagues at Pittsburgh asked physics PhD students and first-year undergrads to sort a pile of physics problems into groups. The students sorted them by what they looked like: this one has a ramp, this one has a pulley. The experts sorted them by the principle underneath: this one's energy conservation, this one's Newton's second law. Same exact problems, completely different piles.</p>
<p>The ramp and the pulley are the legs on the cow. "Our Meta ROAS is down," "our email list isn't growing," and "our new product isn't selling" all look like different problems and very often they are the exact same problem, which is usually that the offer isn't good enough for the audience you're putting it in front of. Once you're sorting by principle instead of by what the problem looks like, you stop solving ramps.</p>
<h3>3. Say so what, out loud, to a human</h3>
<p>This is where physics drops me off and consulting picks me up. A correct answer that nobody can act on is worth nothing, and physicists produce those constantly (I say this with love and a little tongue in cheek). So the third step is translating the toy model back into the life of the actual person I'm talking to. Not "your CAC is up 40%," but "Ads that drive to the homepage instead of deeper into the journey are the thing that's costing you the most, and here's why." If I can't say the so-what in one plain sentence to a founder who's double booked and taking 15m to decide where to allocate resources, then I don't actually understand the problem yet, and I go back to step two.</p>
<h3>4. Rebuild it in chunks</h3>
<p>The last step is putting the legs back on the cow in a way to make it stand on its own. My favorite argument for this is from Herbert Simon, who won a Nobel Prize and also wrote the best story about building things that I've ever read. Two watchmakers, Hora and Tempus, each build watches out of about 1,000 parts. Tempus builds his in one long sequence, so every time the phone rings the whole thing falls apart and he starts over. Hora builds his out of ten-part subassemblies that hold together on their own, so an interruption costs him ten parts, not a thousand. Simon worked out that with the same number of phone calls, Hora finishes roughly 4,000 times more watches. Tempus goes out of business.</p>
<p>Every "now what" I hand a client is a Hora plan. Three things, each one finishable in a week, each one useful even if the other two never happen. Not a 40-step roadmap that collapses the first time somebody gets sick or goes on vacation.</p>
<h2>A worked example that has nothing to do with marketing</h2>
<p>I try a new coffee shop in St. Louis basically every week, so a fair question is whether I'm ever going to run out. Let's Leading Order it.</p>
<p><strong>1. SIZE IT</strong>:<br>The St. Louis metro is around 3 million people, and a coffee shop probably needs somewhere around 10,000 people around it to stay open. So we're talking hundreds of shops, not dozens and not thousands. Already useful.<br><br><strong>2. MAKE THE COW A SPHERE</strong>:<br>Pretend every neighborhood is the same, every shop is the same, and nobody opens or closes. That gets you somewhere around 300 shops, and I'd bet the real number lands right about there.<br><br><strong>3. SAY SO WHAT, OUT LOUD</strong>:<br>At one a week, that's about six years before I'd have to repeat one.<br><br><strong>4. REBUILD IT IN CHUNKS</strong>:<br>Now put the legs back on one at a time and see which ones change the answer. Shops open and close constantly, so a good chunk of those 300 will be gone before I ever get to them. That leg matters. If anything, I need to speed up my coffee shop visits!</p>
<p>The business version is the same shape. An ad account starts bleeding out of nowhere, and nine times out of ten the leading order is one campaign, and inside that campaign it's a handful of ads that got fatigued. You don't need to stare at the whole account, you need to find the sphere.</p>
<p>Anyway, that's how I use physics and cows and consulting lingo to break down problems. Thanks for reading!</p>
